# 04 - Reveals, anticipation, stakes and moments

Research for the Deadly Jenga trailer playbook. Topic: what makes a trailer moment captivating
(reveals, anticipation, stakes, character, humor) and how praised trailers did it.

Method: web research only (no videos watched or downloaded). Everything below is summarized
from written articles, editor blogs, reviews and press coverage. Where a point is my own
inference, it is marked **(inference)**. Where a detail of a trailer comes from someone else's
description, or from general memory rather than a source I read in this session, it is marked
**(second-hand)** or **(memory, unverified)**. See section 7.

The problem this file answers: the first AI cut was a flat sequence with no reveal, no hero, no
stakes and no memorable moment. Every rule below is written so a planner can check it before
rendering.

---

## 1. Rules

Each rule: imperative, testable, why, source.

### A. Hook and clarity

1. **Show gameplay (player-caused action) inside the first 2 seconds; no logos, no slow
   establishing shot first.**
   Test: frame 0-60 contains a character doing something in the game.
   Why: viewers skip intros; most will not wait "half a minute (or even 10 seconds)" to learn
   why they should care. Lieu's Noita and Tactical Breach Wizards trailers open with a few
   seconds of play before anything else. Source: Derek Lieu via Game World Observer;
   Lieu, "Three ways to think of game trailers" (stand-up comedian: the opener must land).

2. **Land the hook (the one thing no other game has) within the first third, and in a 15 s
   cut within the first 5 s.**
   Test: name the hook in one sentence; mark the timestamp where it is first visible.
   For Deadly Jenga the hook is "a giant player's hand pulls blocks out from under tiny
   players." Why: Lieu's order is genre, then hook, then content, and the hook should arrive
   within 10-15 s and no later than the first third. Source: Push to Talk interview with
   Lieu; Road to VR "Formula for great VR trailers" (Hellsweeper opens with ~10 s of its
   distinctive combat before any logo).

3. **Make cause and effect visible in the same frame or across one clean cut.**
   Test: for every "effect" shot (fall, collapse, splash) the cause (pull, wave, gust) is
   either in frame or in the immediately preceding shot.
   Why: Lieu's Arranger breakdown repeats the cause-effect action and even moves objects
   closer than in the real game (2 tiles instead of 5) so the eye does not have to travel.
   Source: Lieu, "Arranger Reveal Trailer".

4. **Show the core mechanic first and plainly, then give it room (at least one full uncut
   shot), then add variations.**
   Test: the first appearance of the pull lasts at least ~1.5 s with no cut mid-action.
   Why: the Sheepherds makeover fixed a rushed trailer by opening with 10 s of plain herding
   and only later adding color/water twists; showing the twist before the basics confused.
   Source: Lieu, "Game Trailer Makeover - Sheepherds".

5. **Show imperfection: someone must fail on screen.**
   Test: at least one survivor falls / one plan fails in the cut.
   Why: showing only perfect herding made Sheepherds look easy; Lieu asked for runs where
   sheep escape. Failure is the stakes. Source: Lieu, Sheepherds makeover.

6. **Never brag in text; let footage prove it.** No "epic", "insane", "mind-blowing" cards.
   Test: no superlatives in any title card.
   Why: Lieu's "first date" analogy - bragging invites doubt. Source: Lieu, "Three ways".

7. **Keep any card to a few plain words; it must read in under a second.**
   Why: flowery end-montage text does not register at speed. Source: Lieu, Hades II review.

### B. Anticipation and payoff

8. **Before any big reveal, give the audience information the characters do not have
   (Hitchcock's bomb), then hold.**
   Test: there is a shot where the viewer sees the threat (hand shadow, receding water,
   wobbling block) while a survivor carries on unaware, lasting at least 1 s.
   Why: Hitchcock to Truffaut: surprise gives a few seconds of shock; suspense, where the
   audience knows about the bomb, gives many seconds of tension. Bordwell's summary: widen
   what the audience knows to build suspense; confine it to share the character's surprise.
   Source: No Film School / Bordwell on Hitchcock-Truffaut.

9. **Effect before cause: show what the threat does to the world before showing the threat.**
   Test: the first sign of the giant (or disaster) is an effect - ripple, shadow, rumble,
   tilt, dust, sound - not the thing itself.
   Why: Jurassic Park's water-glass ripple announces the T-rex with no animal on screen;
   Jaws signals the unseen shark through the barrels it drags, which also tells you its size;
   Shadow of the Colossus first shows only huge hoofed feet and tree-trunk legs, trees
   rattling and dust rising, plus a bellow and stomps you hear before you see. Sources:
   Gizmodo on the ripple effect; Gradesaver / Ebert on Jaws; Kotaku on SotC's first fight.

10. **Sound before sight.** Put the threat's sound (creak, rumble, whoosh, bellow) 0.5-1.5 s
    before its first image.
    Why: in SotC the stomps and rumble come first; in Jaws the motif does the work; analysts
    argue hearing the shark becomes scarier than seeing it. Lieu: "do audio first". Sources:
    Kotaku SotC; Jaws analyses; PC Gamer "The art of making effective game trailers".

11. **Use a held beat (near-silence or a single sustained sound, ~0.5-1 s) right before the
    payoff.**
    Test: the frame before the reveal/impact has the music dropped or thinned.
    Why: contrast; Lieu says lows do not read as low unless there are highs, and music should
    rise and fall rather than stay at one level. Trailer editors place a "hit" on the beat that
    matters and let it ring. Sources: PC Gamer on Lieu; Lieu Hades II review; No Film School
    on trailer sound effects; Frame.io on cutting to a hit. **(Exact 0.5-1 s length is
    inference.)**

12. **Put a reaction shot between the effect and the full reveal: a face looking up.**
    Test: a survivor's face (readable eyes/head direction) turned toward the threat appears
    before or right after the reveal.
    Why: Attack on Titan's first episode shows the colossal hand on the wall and the
    onlookers' frozen awe before anything else; Hannes charges and then stops, horrified.
    The Kuleshov principle: the cut to a face is where the audience reads the emotion.
    Hitchcock's "pure cinema" is close-up, point of view, reaction. Sources: Attack on Titan
    wiki episode recap; StudioBinder/Soundstripe on Kuleshov.

13. **Reveal in stages, never all at once the first time: part, then reaction, then whole.**
    Test: the giant appears at least twice - first partial (fingertips, shadow, a knuckle at
    the top of frame), then whole with scale reference.
    Why: Godzilla (2014) held its monster back deliberately ("cinematic foreplay") and the
    director warned that leading with spectacle makes you plateau; Jaws and Jurassic Park
    delay the full creature. Sources: HuffPost / Cinemovie on Gareth Edwards.

14. **Withholding must be short in a game trailer.** In a 15-30 s piece the star (the hand)
    must be fully seen by the midpoint; withholding is measured in seconds, not acts.
    Why: Godzilla's hour-long delay split critics; Lieu criticizes trailers that hide the
    real game until the end ("dangling keys in front of a baby"). Sources: Push to Talk;
    Godzilla reviews. **(The midpoint limit is inference combining both.)**

### C. Stakes and someone to care about

15. **Establish one survivor as the hero in two seconds: face toward camera, readable color
    or silhouette, doing a small specific action.**
    Test: the hero's first shot is at least 1.5 s, three-quarter or front view, and the same
    hero returns later in the cut.
    Why: Arranger opens by slowly introducing its heroine (asleep, then moving under player
    control by ~5 s); Arizona Sunshine 2's opening shot establishes protagonist, dog companion,
    weapons and tone in one 14 s take. Lieu's Hades II note: introduce main character, motive
    and genre in the first segment. A figure facing away gives no eyes to read, so the reaction
    (rule 12) has nothing to land on **(inference from Kuleshov)**. Sources: Lieu Arranger,
    Arizona Sunshine 2, Hades II posts.

16. **Show vulnerability by scale and by position: put the hero on an edge, a hanging block,
    or the top of a wobbling tower, with ocean below in frame.**
    Test: in the hero's danger shot, the drop (water) is visible.
    Why: Jaws' terror comes from the swimmer's position at water level; SotC keeps Wander small
    and off-center against the landscape. Sources: Ebert on Jaws POV shots; Kotaku on SotC
    camera. **(Application to towers is inference.)**

17. **Include one near miss for the hero (something falls past, the block they stood on is
    pulled, they catch an edge) and let them survive it.**
    Why: suspense needs an outcome in doubt; Bordwell notes Hitchcock's parable leaves open
    whether the diners escape. A near miss is the trailer-length version. **(inference)**

18. **Give the threat a point of view, and give the survivor one, and cut between them.**
    Why: Jaws uses the predator's underwater POV; Godzilla's HALO teaser uses a first-person
    view through the jumper's mask falling toward the monster. Sources: Ebert; Digital Trends
    on the Godzilla teaser.

### D. Scale and power

19. **Always keep a scale reference in a giant shot: a survivor (or several) in the same
    frame as the hand, or a known object (a single block) next to it.**
    Test: every wide shot of the hand contains at least one survivor or block of known size.
    Why: viewers judged the shark by the barrels it towed; Cloverfield's Statue of Liberty head
    reads as huge because it lands in a familiar street (and the feature enlarged it after fans
    said it looked too small). Sources: Jaws analyses; IMDb FAQ / Looper on Cloverfield.

20. **Shoot the giant from low (survivor height) and the survivors from high (giant height).**
    Why: SotC's low camera behind the small hero; Attack on Titan frames the hand over the
    wall from the street. **(The rule is inference generalized from these.)**

21. **Make the giant move slowly and the small things move fast.** Mass reads as slowness.
    **(inference; consistent with SotC's slow-walking colossus and ground shake)**

### E. Fantasy, roles, asymmetry

22. **Show both roles and make it obvious they are both players.** Within the cut, show at
    least one shot from the hand's view and one from a survivor's view of the same event.
    Why: Evolve's trailer showed one full match from all five player views (four hunters and the
    monster), which sold the 4v1 idea; Dead by Daylight's pitch rests on survivors in
    third-person vs killer in first-person. Sources: Gematsu on Evolve's interactive trailer;
    Game Informer on Dead by Daylight.

23. **For VR, show the player's hands/body doing the verb, with a smoothed, stabilized camera.**
    Test: no raw head-cam footage; hand POV shots are smoothed; at least one third-person or
    mixed-reality style shot shows the scale of the hand player.
    Why: raw head motion looks erratic on flat screens; capture at 60 fps, brighten for monitors,
    record at high bitrate. Lieu captured VR at half speed (and moved his head/hands at half
    speed) and insists on a stabilized desktop view. Meta: direct the performer to be expressive.
    Sources: Road to VR; Lieu Arizona Sunshine 2 and Luna posts; Meta MRC guide.

### F. Moments, comedy and endings

24. **Build each 15 s around exactly one "wow" moment, and make that moment one held,
    readable frame** (the screenshot shot): hero small in foreground, threat huge behind, the
    action at its peak, clean silhouette.
    Why: the Cloverfield head, Jurassic ripple and Godzilla HALO jump are each remembered as a
    single image. **(One-per-15 s is inference from these and Lieu's highs-and-lows rule.)**

25. **Vary intensity: quiet, build, peak, release; never one level the whole way.**
    Test: draw the intensity curve; it must have at least one dip before the peak.
    Source: Lieu via PC Gamer; Lieu Hades II review; Godzilla director on plateaus.

26. **Comedy must come from real play and real physics, not staged gags.**
    Why: in Dear Passengers vs Banana Airways, the funny trailer used moments that felt captured
    from real play; the weaker one used a manufactured dance. TABS was marketed through
    hundreds of GIFs of genuine wobbly physics. Sources: Lieu, Dear Passengers vs Banana
    Airways; PC Gamer on TABS.

27. **Time comedy beats: setup (confidence) - turn (the pull) - a held beat on the result -
    a tiny sound - cut.** Put the sound effect exactly on the visual beat; let things exit the
    frame in a funny way.
    Why: Tony Zhou's analysis of Edgar Wright lists funny frame entries/exits, perfectly timed
    sound effects and action synced to music as core visual-comedy tools; comedy editors note
    too-fast and too-slow both kill the joke. Sources: PremiumBeat summary of "How to Do Visual
    Comedy". **(The five-step form is my synthesis.)**

28. **Play danger straight, then undercut it.** Serious framing (slow motion, ominous music)
    on something silly is a proven comedy device.
    Why: Goat Simulator's launch trailer copied the solemn Dead Island trailer with a goat; it
    worked because the parody was also an accurate picture of the absurd game. Source: Kotaku,
    The Mary Sue. Fall Guys' cute look made the mayhem funnier (TechCrunch preview coverage).

29. **End on the strongest beat or on a button (a short, funny or twist shot after the title or
    just before it). Never end on a weak or unclear feature.**
    Why: Lieu "start strong and end strong"; Sheepherds' fixed ending used three visually
    distinct modes, its comic dog bit was the most-rewatched part of the retention graph.
    Cloverfield ended on its single biggest image. Sources: Lieu "Three ways"; Sheepherds;
    Yahoo retrospective on Cloverfield.

### G. What kills interest (negative rules)

30. **Reject the plan if any of these are true:** a generic montage of unrelated wide shots
    with no gameplay readable; any effect without its cause; the hook first appears after the
    first third; every shot is the same intensity and length; no face is ever seen; the threat
    is fully shown in the first second with no buildup; a long framing intro; a shot that
    "goes on for too long" with nothing new happening.
    Sources: Lieu (Sheepherds wide-shot montage "no sense of gameplay"; Banana Airways shot that
    goes on too long; slow intros); Godzilla director on spectacle plateaus.

## 2. Reveal recipes

Timings are for 30 fps. They are working targets **(inference)**, derived from the sources
above (hook in first seconds, staged reveal, held beat, reaction). 15 s and 30 s versions are
given where they differ.

### Recipe 1 - Giant-threat reveal (the hand)
| Step | What | 15 s piece | 30 s piece |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Calm | Hero survivor doing something small and specific, tower in frame | 1.0 s | 2.0 s |
| 2 Effect | Shadow slides over the tower / blocks wobble / creak sound | 1.0 s | 2.0 s |
| 3 Reaction | Hero's face turns up (eyes readable), music drops | 0.7 s | 1.2 s |
| 4 Partial | Fingertips enter top of frame, out of focus or cropped | 0.8 s | 1.5 s |
| 5 Held beat | Fingers close on a block; near-silence | 0.5 s | 0.8 s |
| 6 Payoff | Pull: block slides out, tower lurches, survivors scramble; music hits | 1.5 s | 2.5 s |
| 7 Scale wide | Whole hand plus tower plus tiny survivors in one frame | 1.0 s | 2.0 s |
Total ~6.5 s (15 s) / ~12 s (30 s).

### Recipe 2 - Disaster reveal (tsunami or tornado)
1. Wrong calm: survivors busy on the tower, ocean flat (1 s).
2. Effect before cause: the sea pulls back from the island / clouds start to spin / blocks
   rattle in wind (1-1.5 s). Sound first: low roar under music.
3. One survivor notices; points or freezes (0.7 s).
4. Wide: the wave or funnel against the horizon with the tower for scale (1.5 s).
5. Held beat: impact frame held a fraction before contact (0.5 s).
6. Impact and consequence: survivors knocked off, blocks scattered (1.5-2 s).

### Recipe 3 - Hero survives (near miss)
1. Hero established (face, color) on a block (1 s).
2. Threat targets that block - hand grips it or wave rises behind (1 s).
3. Block goes; hero drops, catches an edge or jumps (1 s, consider 50% slow motion).
4. Held beat on the dangling hero, other survivors falling past (0.7 s).
5. Hero pulls up; small victory gesture or a look at the hand (1 s).
Optional button: the hand wiggles a finger at them / taps the next block (0.8 s).

### Recipe 4 - Comedy fail beat
1. Confidence: a survivor makes a bold jump, climb or taunt (1 s).
2. Turn: the hand pulls the exact block they are going to (0.5 s).
3. The fall, played straight - wide, so the full arc into the ocean is visible (1-1.5 s).
4. Held beat with near-silence (0.4-0.6 s).
5. Tiny splash sound exactly on the visual; cut on the splash or on the next frame (0.3 s).
6. Optional reaction: the hand gives a little wave, or another survivor looks at camera.

### Recipe 5 - Two-roles mirror cut (asymmetry)
1. Hand player's view: VR hands reaching down at the tiny tower (1.5 s, smoothed).
2. Match cut on the same instant: survivor's view looking up at the huge fingers (1.5 s).
3. Wide third-person showing both at once (1.5 s).
Rule: the same block, same moment, both views. This tells flat viewers both are real players.

### Recipe 6 - Ending with a button
1. Peak: the biggest collapse or wave (2-3 s).
2. Title card over a quiet aftermath shot (2 s).
3. Button after the title: one small, funny, true shot (last survivor clinging on, or a
   survivor bobbing in the ocean who waves) (1.5-2 s). Cut on the gag, not after it.

## 3. Case studies

Each: hook, structure in order, key moment and setup/payoff, how gameplay was made clear, what
people said. "(second-hand)" means I read a description, not the trailer. "(memory,
unverified)" means from general knowledge, not a source read in this session.

### Games

**1. Untitled Goose Game - reveal trailer (2017).**
Hook: the premise in one line, a "horrible goose" causing trouble in a village (the tagline).
Structure (second-hand): plain gameplay of the goose stealing and honking at villagers, set to
piano music (Debussy came later partly because of the clip's reception). Key moment: the goose
annoys a person who reacts - the comedy is cause (honk/steal) and effect (human reaction) in one
frame. Clarity: you are the goose; the to-do list implies goals. Reception: went viral on social
media; critics called the tagline a premise that sells itself. Lesson: a clear villain-protagonist
fantasy with a reacting victim; play mischief straight. Sources: Wikipedia; Pedestrian; Aure Review.

**2. Goat Simulator - launch trailer (2014).**
Hook: recognizable parody - it copies the reversed, slow-motion, solemn structure of the famous
Dead Island trailer (second-hand; Dead Island's reverse-chronology/slow-mo/piano form is
memory, unverified) but with a goat, a jetpack and an exploding petrol station. Key moment:
serious music on absurd ragdoll physics. Clarity: critics said the parody was probably an
accurate picture of the game. Lesson for Deadly Jenga: candy tone plus genuinely dramatic
framing is funnier than winking. Sources: Kotaku; The Mary Sue; MCV.

**3. Fall Guys - E3 2019 reveal.**
Hook: dozens of jelly-bean contestants in a game-show obstacle course (coverage: "100-player"
chaotic race, "Takeshi's Castle" comparisons). Structure (memory, unverified): crowd at start line,
quick cuts of obstacle rounds, mass falls, eliminations, crown. Key moment: many tiny characters
knocked off by huge obstacles - the scale and comedy of mass falling. Critics noted cute graphics
make the mayhem funnier; Shacknews complained it explained little - spectacle carried it.
Lesson: falling tiny characters are inherently funny; but you still need a goal (win the crown /
survive). Sources: DualShockers (fetch failed; title only), TechCrunch, Shacknews.

**4. Evolve - 4v1 interactive trailer (2014).**
Hook: one full match viewable from six channels - four hunters, the monster, and a combined
commentary view. Key move: the same event from both sides. Lesson: for an asymmetric game,
the viewer must see that the monster is a player, not an AI boss. Source: Gematsu; Game Informer.

**5. Dead by Daylight - reveal (2016).**
Hook: asymmetric horror; survivors in third-person, the killer in first-person. The first
in-game teaser (second-hand) showed an injured survivor crawling while the killer stalks.
Key moment: vulnerability (injured, crawling) with the threat present. Lesson: different camera
grammars per role make the two roles instantly distinct - for Deadly Jenga, hand = first-person
VR, survivors = third-person. Sources: Game Informer; PCGamesN.

**6. Superhot - Kickstarter trailer (2014).**
Hook: one rule, "time moves only when you move", shown, not explained. Destructoid said it laid
out how the mechanic works well. Key moment: frozen bullets that move when the player moves -
cause and effect in a single shot. Lesson: when the mechanic is novel, show it in one clean shot
first (rule 4). Sources: Destructoid; Kotaku; PC Gamer.

**7. Totally Accurate Battle Simulator - GIF-led marketing.**
Hook: wobbly physics producing ridiculous battles. Landfall's CEO posted hundreds of GIFs on
Twitter; PC Gamer's writer made about a hundred himself. Lesson: each comedy moment should work
as a 3-6 s loop on its own - setup, wobble, result. Sources: PC Gamer; Vice; Game Developer.

**8. Teardown - Gamescom 2020 reveal.**
Hook: fully destructible voxel world inside a heist - plan a route by smashing walls, then race
an alarm. Developer said the destruction came first and the setting was fitted to it. Lesson:
destruction alone is a toy; a goal and a clock make it a game (stakes). Sources: Gematsu;
Game Developer; PC Gamer.

**9. Beat Saber - mixed-reality trailers.**
Hook: a person slicing blocks to music, filmed from outside so flat viewers see the body move.
The teaser that went viral was reportedly a simple prototype clip, not the big planned trailer.
LIV/green-screen capture became standard. Lesson: for VR, show the player's body doing the verb
from outside, plus in-world view. Sources: Voices of VR (via search summary); Meta MRC guide;
GamesBeat on making MR trailers.

**10. Arizona Sunshine 2 - launch trailer (Derek Lieu).**
Hook: one 14 s opening take that states four facts: VR game with a voiced hero, a dog
companion, zombie gunplay, light humor (throwing a ball, shooting zombies, waving at the dog).
Captured in-game at half speed, about 60 takes. Key lesson: a single choreographed shot can
establish hero, companion, verb and tone together; smoothed desktop view is essential.
Source: Lieu blog.

**11. Arranger - reveal (Derek Lieu).**
Hook: player input visibly moves the heroine and drags objects by ~5 s. Mechanic repeated for
clarity; objects moved closer than in-game to reduce eye travel; montage cuts matched on
eye-trace; title card built from the core mechanic. Lesson: rules 3, 4, 15. Source: Lieu blog.

**12. Sheepherds - trailer makeover (Derek Lieu).**
Original: barking intro, title cut in before the payoff, mechanics rushed, cosmetics at 23 s.
Fixed: 10 s of plain herding up front, each twist given a full shot, imperfect runs, cosmetics
after the halfway point, a comic dog bit (the most rewatched part), ending on three visually
distinct modes. Lesson: rules 1, 4, 5, 29. Source: Lieu blog.

**13. Dear Passengers vs Banana Airways (Derek Lieu comparison).**
Same premise, very different results. Winner: many short, varied, captured-from-play comic
moments (about 15 scenarios), cute characters. Loser: slow framing intro, a wide repair shot that
goes on too long, staged dance gag, stutter and too-clean voice chat. Lesson: rules 26, 30.
Source: Lieu blog.

**14. Gorilla Tag and Among Us - counter-examples.**
Both grew mainly through creators (TikTok/YouTube for Gorilla Tag; Korean and Brazilian
creators, then Sodapoppin's group on Twitch, for Among Us), not a famous trailer. What spread
was short clips of real social moments: a chase, an accusation, a betrayal. Lesson: the shareable
unit is a 5-10 s human moment with a reaction, which is what a trailer moment should imitate.
Sources: PC Gamer; Kotaku; Meta developer blog on Another Axiom.

**15. Half-Life: Alyx - announcement (2019).**
Coverage highlighted hands-on moments (scanner in one hand, gun in the other) - the VR verb
shown through hands. Commenters found floating hands odd. Lesson: for Deadly Jenga's hand
player, the hands are the character; make them expressive and well-lit (second-hand).
Sources: GameSpot; forum reaction summarized by search.

### Film and other reference reveals

**Jaws (1975).** Malfunctioning shark forced restraint: barrels, underwater POV and the musical
motif stand in for the shark; the full shark comes late. Lessons: effect before cause; the
effect (barrels) also tells size; POV of the threat. Sources: Gradesaver; Ebert; No Film School.

**Jurassic Park (1993).** Teaser showed no dinosaurs - only a mosquito in amber and the logo.
In the film, ripples in a cup announce the T-rex before it appears. Lesson: a small, familiar
object reacting to something huge is the strongest anticipation image. Sources: Nerdist;
Gizmodo; Gigazine.

**Godzilla (2014) - HALO teaser.** Soldiers jump into a burning city with red flares, Ligeti's
choral Requiem (the 2001 monolith music), a first-person view through a jumper's mask, glimpses
of spines through smoke. Director: held the monster back as "cinematic foreplay" and avoided
the spectacle plateau. Lesson: human POV falling toward the giant; partial glimpses; eerie sound.
Sources: Maxim; Digital Trends; WhatCulture; HuffPost.

**Cloverfield (2007 teaser).** Handheld party footage interrupted; the Statue of Liberty's head
lands in the street; ends with only a date. Lesson: a familiar object as scale reference; withhold
the monster; end on the single biggest image. Sources: Yahoo retrospective; Looper; IMDb FAQ.

**Attack on Titan (episode 1).** A giant hand grips the top of the wall above the town; people
stare frozen; then the face appears and the gate is kicked in. Lesson: hand first, reaction,
then the rest - directly applicable to a giant hand game. Source: Attack on Titan wiki recap.

**Shadow of the Colossus (first colossus).** Long quiet approach; alone; sound first (bellow,
stomps, controller rumble); first view is only feet and legs; camera low behind a small
off-center hero. Lesson: isolation, sound first, partial view, low camera. Source: Kotaku; BFI.

## 4. Applied to Deadly Jenga - five moment designs

Shot lengths in seconds. "S" = survivor, "H" = hand player. Hero survivor = one specific, brightly
colored survivor ("Mint", inference: pick the most readable color on the candy palette) who
recurs across moments.

### Moment 1 - The hand reveal (7.0 s)
1. (1.2 s) Close-medium, three-quarter front: Mint climbs onto a block near the top, ocean
   sparkling below. Light music. Mint looks pleased.
2. (1.0 s) Same framing; a soft shadow slides across Mint and the blocks. Low creak/whoosh sound
   starts before the shadow.
3. (0.7 s) Close on Mint's face turning up. Music drops out.
4. (0.8 s) Low angle from Mint's position: blurry giant fingertips enter the top of frame.
5. (0.6 s) Held beat: the fingers close on the block next to Mint; near-silence, one wood/candy
   creak.
6. (1.5 s) The pull: block slides out, tower lurches, two other survivors tumble; music hits.
7. (1.2 s) Wide: whole hand over the whole tower, tiny survivors visible - the screenshot frame.

### Moment 2 - Tsunami reveal (7.5 s)
1. (1.2 s) Wide, low: survivors climbing, flat ocean, gulls.
2. (1.3 s) The ocean visibly pulls back from the island; deep roar under the music (effect
   before cause).
3. (0.8 s) A survivor stops and points toward the horizon (reaction, face visible).
4. (1.5 s) Wide from behind the tower: wall of water on the horizon, tower in frame for scale.
5. (0.5 s) Held beat: water frozen just before contact, near-silence.
6. (2.2 s) Impact: survivors swept off, blocks scattered; Mint clings to the top block.

### Moment 3 - Hero survives (6.0 s)
1. (1.0 s) Mint mid-tower, front view.
2. (1.0 s) The hand grips Mint's block (Mint in frame for scale).
3. (1.2 s) Block yanked; Mint falls, catches the edge of the block below (50% slow motion).
4. (0.8 s) Held beat: Mint dangling, another survivor falls past into the ocean.
5. (1.2 s) Mint pulls up and looks straight up at the hand.
6. (0.8 s) Button: the hand gives a slow "I see you" finger wag.

### Moment 4 - Comedy fail (4.5 s)
1. (1.0 s) A survivor runs and takes a confident leap toward a block.
2. (0.5 s) The hand slides that exact block out mid-jump.
3. (1.4 s) Wide: the survivor's full arc into the ocean, played straight (no music sting yet).
4. (0.5 s) Held near-silence.
5. (0.3 s) Tiny "plip" splash on the visual frame. Cut.
6. (0.8 s) The hand holding the stolen block, giving a small wave.

### Moment 5 - Two roles, one block, and the ending button (7.5 s)
1. (1.5 s) H's VR view (smoothed): their hands reaching down to the tiny candy tower, a block
   pinched between fingers.
2. (1.5 s) Match cut on the same instant: S's view looking up at the same giant fingers on the
   same block.
3. (1.5 s) Third-person wide showing both - the hand player's hands and the tower - so viewers
   understand both are players.
4. (1.5 s) The tower collapses into the ocean; title card over the splash.
5. (1.5 s) Button after the title: Mint bobbing alone in the water, looks at camera, then a
   single block lands on Mint's head with a "bonk". Cut.

## 5. Pre-render checklist (yes/no)

Score the planned sequence. Any "no" in items 1-12 must be fixed before rendering.

1. Is a character doing a game action within the first 2 s (no logo/establishing shot first)?
2. Is the hook (giant hand pulling blocks under tiny players) visible within the first third
   (first 5 s of a 15 s cut)?
3. Is there one named hero survivor whose face is seen within the first 3 s and who returns later?
4. Does every effect (fall, collapse, splash) have its cause in frame or in the previous shot?
5. Is the first appearance of the pull at least 1.5 s and uncut?
6. Does the threat's first appearance come as an effect (shadow, ripple, wobble, sound) before
   the threat itself?
7. Does the threat's sound start before its first image?
8. Is there a reaction shot (a face turned toward the threat) around the reveal?
9. Is there a held beat (music drop / near-silence, ~0.5 s) before the biggest payoff?
10. Is the hand shown in stages (partial first, whole later) and fully seen by the midpoint?
11. Does every wide shot of the hand include survivors or a block for scale?
12. Does someone fail (fall) on screen, and does the hero survive a near miss?
13. Are both roles shown as players (a hand-player view and a survivor view of the same event)?
14. Are VR/hand shots smoothed, 60 fps, and bright enough for a monitor?
15. Is there exactly one clear "wow" frame per ~15 s that would work as a screenshot?
16. Does the intensity curve dip at least once before the peak?
17. Is at least one comedy beat timed setup - turn - hold - sound - cut, from real physics?
18. Do the ocean/drop and the danger stay visible in the hero's danger shots?
19. Does the cut end on the strongest beat or a button shot (not a weak feature)?
20. Are all cards a few plain words, with no superlatives?
21. Is no shot longer than it needs to be (each shot shows something new)?
22. Are shots different from each other in scale, angle or action (not a generic montage)?
23. Is the tone both candy-bright and genuinely perilous (real falls, real waves)?
24. Could a viewer say, after one watch, what they would do in each role?
