FESTIVAL CITY SHAPE IMPLEMENTATION STUDY
23 October 2026. Actual Unity 6000.3 tests plus source/architecture analysis.

RESULT: PACKING WON THIS RUN; FEWER MESH COLLIDERS DID NOT
The packed copy reduced wave median frame time from 156.42ms to 124.26ms (6.4 to 8.0 FPS). The mesh copy reduced collider count but measured 188.25ms (5.3 FPS). Prefer the packed copy for further testing. Both copies are preserved; neither replaces the baseline. Full counts, timings and qualifications are in Results.txt and Validation.txt.

PRESERVATION AND SCOPE
FestivalCity-before-shape-study.zip preserves the original city prefab, meta files, map implementation and prior evidence before this task's edits. Original Festival of Giants - City remains selectable. Two additional copies use the same layout: Festival City - Packed Shapes and Festival City - Surface Mesh. Neither changes the city silhouette, dimensions, structural member positions, room openings, ramp geometry, masses, gravity, solver settings or number of independent falling pieces. The variants are explicit map choices; neither silently replaces the original.
World/Maps and DevTools/Editor changed. The existing lobby catalog received two generated entries. No Core, Characters, Presentation, Packages or ProjectSettings changes were made for this task. Other existing local edits are preserved.

WHAT ACTUALLY EXISTS
The city has 633 movable bodies but 1,049 physical collision shapes. It contains 532 ordinary one-shape pieces and 101 new compound/flight pieces. Those 101 pieces account for 517 physical shapes: 467 boxes and 50 convex ramp meshes.
The existing architecture duplicates each physical shape as a player-facing collider. This isolates CharacterController contact from the dynamic structural simulation and lets collision queries follow displayed/interpolated poses. Therefore the baseline effectively has 2,098 map-related collision shapes: 1,049 structural plus 1,049 survivor shapes, excluding foundation/actors and other scenery.
The 101 new pieces alone have 517 separate survivor objects. Their physical shapes also have 416 unnecessary child transforms: only one physical shape originally sits directly on each member root. This is a concrete implementation cost, not an unavoidable property of the architecture.
The census counted 2,217 GameObjects inside the baseline map hierarchy; player proxies live outside that hierarchy and are additional objects. Do not confuse this count with independent physics bodies.

EXPERIMENT A: PACK IDENTICAL SHAPES ONTO FEWER OBJECTS
Each manufactured member can hold all its axis-aligned BoxCollider components on its existing Rigidbody GameObject. Collider.center stores each part's local position. Its player copy similarly holds all the room's colliders on one proxy object. A ramp already has its own body and convex mesh, so its exact slope is retained.
This removes 416 physical child GameObjects and 416 survivor GameObjects, or 832 in total. Map hierarchy count falls from 2,217 to 1,801; new-section survivor objects fall from 517 to 101. New-section proxy pose writes fall from up to 517 to 101 per change. Total physical shape count stays 1,049; this primarily removes object/transform bookkeeping rather than native contact constraints.
The original materials, visible model, friction, mass calculation and fracture units remain. Existing Build callers retain their old behavior because the new parameters are optional and default off. No change to collision ownership/Climbables contracts was needed.
This is the lowest-risk tested option. It cannot remove the intrinsic cost of 633 simultaneously active bodies, many contacts, bot planning or several physics catch-up steps per rendered frame.

EXPERIMENT B: ONE CONCAVE PLAYER SURFACE PER ROOM
Keep the packed boxes for actual dynamic building collisions. For each complex member, combine its original collision box surfaces into one shared, pre-generated concave triangle mesh on the existing kinematic survivor-proxy system. The 50 simple flight proxies retain their convex ramp meshes. The mesh is cached by authored geometry and never deformed/re-cooked each frame; only the owning proxy pose moves.
New-section player shapes fall from 517 to 101. Whole-map player shapes fall from 1,049 to 633; structural shapes stay 1,049. Total map-related shapes therefore fall from 2,098 to 1,682, about 19.8%, while preserving all 633 falling members. This is not an 80% reduction of the entire physics workload: 80.5% applies only to the new sections' survivor shapes/objects.
Unity supports concave MeshColliders on kinematic bodies; they are only used against convex character collision here, not against other concave structures. This avoids asking PhysX to solve an unsupported dynamic concave room.
Tradeoffs: a triangle mesh has a different query cost from a few boxes and is not automatically faster. It is also a surface representation: deep penetration recovery, edge normals, one-sided triangles and unusual teleport-inside-wall cases can differ from primitive solid-volume tests. Static walking success is necessary but does not prove identical recovery in every moving-wall/VR-hand case. Keep this copy experimental until moving/rotating contact, deep overlap, climbing, vaulting and actual Quest tests establish equivalence. Do not install it globally solely because the collider count is smaller.

WHY A SINGLE DYNAMIC ROOM COLLIDER IS NOT A FREE FIX
A room with several separated floors and an open interior is concave. A single convex hull would fill the atrium and spaces between galleries, producing invisible obstructions. A single concave mesh cannot be a freely simulated non-kinematic Rigidbody in this Unity/PhysX setup. Four separate floors cannot be collapsed into one ordinary box without filling the spaces between them. Reducing the number of decorative window polygons would not help collision cost: those glazed panels already have no collider.
The two representations can legitimately differ: simple convex pieces for building-to-building dynamics, accurate concave surfaces for kinematic character queries. Their separate responsibilities are what makes Experiment B legal; it does not eliminate the physical wall/floor boxes.

OTHER OPTIONS, ORDERED BY VALUE AND CONSEQUENCE
1. Unify character collision with the real structural geometry.
This is the largest plausible geometry-preserving reduction: eliminating the duplicate layer would cut this map's map-related shape count from about 2,098 to 1,049, remove the proxy objects/bodies and remove pose-copy work. It needs a dedicated prototype, not a blanket layer toggle. Unity's standard CharacterController does not automatically push rigidbodies; that weakens the assumption that a duplicate layer is fundamentally required merely to avoid player pushing.
However, our movement, climbing, vaulting, ledge look and bot ground queries explicitly exclude Block or require Surface. All must agree on the new query policy. We also need to preserve support carry and interpolated visual alignment. Unity's CCT overlap recovery treats dynamic objects differently from static ones, so moving/crushing walls and initially overlapping capsules require particular attention. Verify a real player cannot shift a resting tower before removing the one-way layer. This is a promising larger refactor, not a demonstrated safe optimization in these tests.
2. Spatially bound the shared survivor groups.
CastleSurfaceGroup already uses spatial cells. CityStructuralMember currently fills groups by insertion order with a capacity of 24 members. Such a group can span many floors, or even bridge distant buildings when one group fills partway through another building. Smaller spatial groups could reduce compound bounds and unnecessary query candidates while retaining every surface. More kinematic groups also have a cost; benchmark queries and total frame time before picking cell dimensions. Reindexing or moving individual proxy bodies must not add more update cost than it saves. This is unmeasured in the two copies above.
3. Bake/query a navigation description instead of repeatedly rediscovering every collision face.
The room compiler already knows floors, landings, ramps and exits. It can emit immutable local walkable patches and adjacency and update only the owning member's pose/state. This keeps the identical city and physics while reducing bots' geometry scanning and graph growth. The proper design crosses the World/Characters boundary through capability data in Core; no cross-box class references. A new shared contract would need approval before implementation. Moving/destroyed surfaces must invalidate affected routes, and actual motor collision remains authoritative. Do not replace the bot motor with teleports.
4. Batch room artwork separately from collision.
New room meshes use five material/submesh slots and ordinary MeshRenderers. They do not register with the current CandyCrateVisual batching path, which batches only eligible one-submesh crate visuals. Repeated room models already share cached geometry, so per-model/per-material instancing or a compatible palette shader is plausible. Render groups must allow individual moving/collapsing members, retain glass/emission distinctions and cull independently; baking the entire city into one mesh would remove that flexibility. An exact draw-call/GPU profile is needed to quantify this. No renderer/material change was made in this task.
5. Selective per-member solver and collision-mode budgets.
Only test lower iteration counts on the large manufactured pieces, leaving critical thin bearings or unstable stacks at their required settings. Their 10m solid sections may be less sensitive than the earlier small-block cathedral. Historical evidence in BlockPhysicsPipelineAudit.txt showed that lowering cathedral iterations destabilized it, so those results do not justify a global reduction. Continuous collision work should be concentrated on fast/thin-risk members, including rotation, not blindly disabled on all falling objects. Both study copies retain the baseline 20/8 and CCD policy so this comparison stays interpretable.
6. Simplify non-bearing detail for structural contacts while retaining player boundaries.
Some balcony parapets need to stop a player but need not necessarily participate in expensive falling-building contacts. There are only 24 small Hall parapet boxes, so even removing every one from structural physics would save about 2.3% of structural shapes. Most remaining boxes are real floors, walls or bearing ledges. Removing their collision can change load paths, allow pieces to clip, or make a supported ramp fall. This option offers smaller gains than the object packing and should use an explicit contact-purpose classification, not a visual-size heuristic.
7. Merge columns, slabs and adjacent rooms into larger manufactured assemblies.
Appearance and walk routes can remain identical, but pieces will break into larger chunks and torque/inertia/load transfer will change. Keeping the same number of colliders under fewer bodies does not necessarily reduce contacts; older resting-aggregate experiments already made some fixtures slower. Compare native step cost, actual wave cost, loss-of-support response and fun before choosing assembly size. This is a moderate gameplay change, not an exact equivalent of our existing 633-member collapse.
8. Supported static/kinematic city with dynamic release on failure.
This could save much more during quiet play by keeping most of a city outside full rigidbody solving, then releasing affected assemblies. But it requires a real support/load graph, contact-area/center-of-mass checks, impact propagation and cascaded activation. Simple 'still connected to ground' tests can hold up impossible cantilevers. It changes the physical model, introduces state/network complexity and can front-load a large CPU spike at release. It is a separate framework experiment, not a small collider optimization.
9. Cheap distant debris after failure.
Retire only genuinely disposable displaced debris with safe player/hand proximity and swept collision checks. Large intact occupied rooms must remain solid. The existing ordinary-block cleanup/ballistic pipeline is not automatically installed on every new room section. A room-aware adapter could reduce the tail of a disaster, but should preserve member indices and never delete a player-occupied structure because its velocity is high. This changes distant interactions and does not solve idle geometry/query overhead.

HOW TO CHOOSE
Start with exact packing. Use the mesh copy as an A/B experiment rather than an assumed universal upgrade. The highest-value next architecture investigation is removing or localizing the duplicated character collision layer, with correct recovery/moving-support tests. In parallel, profile the room renderer and bot geometry work; neither is fixed just by replacing collision shapes.
Preserve a physics budget in terms of active contacts, proxy updates, queries and rendering as well as bodies. A 24% increase in body count can create a much larger increase in work if compound shapes, proxies, query candidates and fixed-step catch-up also increase. Shape reduction is worthwhile, but it is not evidence that a 6 FPS stress scene can reach 72 FPS without addressing those other costs.

MEASUREMENT LIMITS
Fresh same-session baseline and copies, 32 production bots, actual seeded tsunami, unchanged force/height. Idle samples 3 wall seconds; bots 4; wave 25 after a 2s lead-in. Walkability uses the production movement motor with scripted commands and .02s substeps at 2x clock. Initial placement per route is the only teleport. Autonomous ascent/objective completion is not claimed. Native settling/support-loss checks are real isolated PhysX simulations. Short unfocused Editor timings remain affected by other processes, caching, physics contact order and variable bot decisions; no causally precise standalone/Quest speedup is asserted.
Quest, XR tracked-hand edge cases, deep mesh penetration recovery and live two-player networking were not tested. Do not extrapolate the FPS table to a headset.
