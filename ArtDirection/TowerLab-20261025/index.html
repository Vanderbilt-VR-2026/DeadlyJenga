The important translation from architecture to this game

Use architecture for load paths and spatial ideas, not as evidence that loose blocks will stand. Habitat 67 combines modules into a connected system using post-tensioning, rods, cables and welding. Its attractive offsets do not establish that the same shapes can simply be stacked loose. My proposal instead places broad bearings under balanced rigid cassettes. Safdie Architects — Habitat 67.

The Interlace demonstrates stacked inhabited bars and roof-level shared spaces. I borrow the idea that a building block can contain an entire neighborhood, then constrain each game bar to a valid centre crossing and an independently checked upper-stack centre of mass. Büro Ole Scheeren — The Interlace.

SOM describes Burj Khalifa around a buttressed core; CTBUH describes outriggers engaging perimeter columns to reduce deformation. PhysX rigid bodies do not simulate a concrete core bending or a loose decorative truss stiffening a tower. In the proposed Sky Tree, the three wings are actually part of the rigid section. A loose belt laid around separate columns is not credited as a tension tie. SOM — Burj Khalifa · CTBUH — Outrigger Design Guide preview.

Arup's CCTV system is a continuous tube supported through its surface grid. The Kissing Towers proposal is a different mechanism: compression at the crown with thrust resisted at the feet. It is deliberately not a loose replica of CCTV, and it has high risk until a small native prototype works. Arup — CCTV structural system.

Unity supports several colliders acting together under one body, but explicitly recommends testing compound arrangements for behavior and cost. This motivates four-storey cassettes, not the assumption that they are free: contact shapes, active contacts, queries and body bounds all still matter. Unity — compound collider construction · Unity — enhanced physics performance.

NVIDIA notes that difficult joint convergence is associated with heavy/light chains and large mass ratios; its joint guidance recommends avoiding ratios above 10 in those scenarios. This is relevant to Bookfall's hinges, not a universal rule for every loose block contact. Large wings on tiny jointed props are an experiment to reject early. NVIDIA PhysX — joints and convergence.

The public Red Faction session overview explicitly treats destruction as something that changes level flow, strategy and exploits, and identifies playtesting and technical constraints. I used that as the design question: does the damaged tower create another playable place? I did not treat its engine or talk overview as a ready-made Unity implementation, and did not claim to have watched unavailable lecture content. GDC 2010 — Luke Schneider, Multiplayer Level Design in Red Faction Guerrilla (session overview).

Embark's July 2025 destruction update explicitly discusses keeping level design playable after substantial destruction, alongside knock-on building damage and clearer damage visuals. My interpretation is to judge each tower in several damaged states, not just approve its intact silhouette. A leaning section should create cover or a route choice, while cues let players understand the danger. This is a design lesson, not evidence that our physics engine has the same destruction features. Embark — THE FINALS destruction update, July 2025.

In the published Building Creator breakdown, Embark technical artist Adrian Björkerud describes pre-fractured assets, watertight source geometry, authored collision, shared building parameters and reusable feature nodes. That is a production pipeline beyond ordinary loose Rigidbody stacks. The relevant adaptation here is a reusable cassette recipe: load-bearing shell, floors, circulation openings, landing pads, collision and visual layer must agree. If finer fracture is added later, pre-author a small bounded set of fragments and its collision; do not assume a runtime cut can cheaply manufacture a playable interior. This proposal does not require Houdini or a package change. Embark technical artist Adrian Björkerud / SideFX — Building Creator.

A quick physics screen before opening Unity

For a rigid upper assembly on horizontal seats, project its combined centre of mass onto the support polygon formed by real contact patches. For every separable level, use the mass of everything above that level; checking only one cassette misses unstable upper stacks. A point inside the polygon is only a static feasibility screen. It does not guarantee stability under impacts, sliding, contact tolerances or moving players.

x̄ = Σ(mᵢ xᵢ) / Σmᵢ. For a simplified rigid assembly accelerated laterally at its centre of mass, tipping begins near a / g ≈ b / h, where b is the horizontal margin from the COM projection to the active support edge and h is its height. Example: a uniform 60 m tower with COM at 30 m and a 10 m margin gives approximately 0.33 g; narrowing that margin to 5 m gives 0.17 g. Those are illustrative mechanics calculations, not a tsunami prediction. A wave applying force at another height has a different moment arm, and sliding can occur first.

Practical consequence: use a real wide base, taper or an inhabited buttress to keep height affordable. A bigger decorative island or a painted foundation does not widen the tower's support. Do not silently move the centre of mass below the visible structure to make a failed concept appear successful.

For Bookfall, a 22 m wing rotated 65° from vertical has outward reach 22 sin65° ≈ 19.9 m and endpoint rise 22 cos65° ≈ 9.3 m. The receiving roof must be designed for that geometry. A roof elsewhere is not a guaranteed landing. These calculations are checked by the build script.

Rules for making height playable

  • Many useful levels, not just one faraway rooftop. Aim for 3 m floor intervals in the first gray box, rest courts every three or four levels and views between levels. Do not force a continuous 60 m climb as the only route.
  • Two routes should differ in cost. One reliable stair/ramp route; one climb, short drop or cross-tower route. After a local failure, try to preserve one escape rather than guarantee every route remains open.
  • Build the stair opening as part of the section. Start with roughly 1.5–2 m clear route width, generous headroom relative to the current avatar, 20–28° ramp targets and 1.5–2 m flat turns. These are gray-box targets, not verified universal comfort limits.
  • Flush exterior climb strips. Recess red floors and decorative bands behind designated wall/rib faces. Make a deliberate top-out platform. Repeated proud floor lips are a regression to the festival problem.
  • No tiny surprise steps at slope ends. In the current source the default player scale is 0.5; CharacterController step offset is 0.05 × scale, only 2.5 cm. The Foundry tests exposed both upper and lower landing failures. A ramp must finish onto a flat pad before a turn or an adjacent vertical collider.
  • Space must exist after damage too. Reserve fall lanes, side exits from cages, a route outside a Drop-Stop crush zone, and broad footholds on tilted remains. Keep objectives away from the sole part that can vanish.
  • VR and bots are separate acceptance checks. A diagrammed route is not a capsule sweep, a successful capsule sweep is not tracked-hand comfort, and a few bots moving is not proof they navigate every floor. The game also allows equipment that blocks climbing/jumping; objective-critical routes must account for that.

First prototype · Lower risk

01 Sky Tree

Study height 72 m · 24 vertical levels · footprint 34 × 34 m

Open full vector structure diagramOpen full vector mechanism diagram

The build

Six four-storey Y-shaped cassettes. Three deep spine walls form both the load path and the edges of open vertical courtyards. Floors sit between the spines rather than projecting past their outer climbing faces.

What is different

A very tall, apparently complicated tower is mostly six rigid inhabited sections. The spaces between its three wings supply rooms, cover and vertical views without constructing separate rooms from small blocks.

How it could stand

The three spine ends must meet broad, aligned bearing patches on the cassette below. The upper assembly's projected centre of mass must remain inside their support triangle. Visual wings do not count as support unless their colliders actually touch.

How people move through it

An internal switchback links 3 m floor intervals; exterior spine strips offer a separate climb-and-rest route. Every third or fourth level opens into a sky court with at least two exits. Stair slots and turning pads belong to the rigid cassette and remain aligned if it tilts.

What damage changes

Removing a bearing shoe first tilts or unseats one cassette. Its floors and spine can bridge onto a neighboring wing while other sections remain usable. A single cassette cannot crumble internally unless a later, bounded fracture system is deliberately added.

Cost and compromises

Few moving sections, but their internal floors and stairs still need many simple shapes. Avoid one collider per decorative rib. Do not make all six cassettes one body or every small hit becomes an all-or-nothing event.

Smallest useful test

Check one-, three- and six-cassette versions. Remove each of the three shoes in turn, test a player on the outermost floor, then hit at lower and mid-height. Reject spontaneous seating creep or an outer climb strip interrupted by a floor lip.

First prototype · Lower–medium risk

02 Zipper Spire

Study height 60 m · 18 vertical levels · footprint 30 × 24 m

Open full vector structure diagramOpen full vector mechanism diagram

The build

Six mirrored three-floor folded sections alternate their long side. A broad central bearing spine carries the stack, while deep occupied wings and stair streets form a broken zigzag skyline.

What is different

The dramatic offset is partly a silhouette, not a truly off-balance load. A heavier spine and a shorter opposite wing keep each section's centre of mass over its bearing while the exposed stair street reads as a diagonal city climbing into the sky.

How it could stand

Author the actual collider mass distribution and measure it. Alternating silhouettes do not cancel an unstable intermediate stage automatically. Both the complete stack and every upper sub-stack need a valid support polygon.

How people move through it

A wide diagonal stair street ascends one face, then crosses a flat sheltered landing before reversing. A second route uses the straight spine face. Keep balcony edges recessed and widen turns rather than adding little red shelves outside the wall.

What damage changes

An unseated folded cassette becomes a large sloping street with a sheltered underside. Neighboring offsets can catch it and create a new traverse. Catching is a desired possibility, not a guaranteed scripted landing.

Cost and compromises

The folded shell can be one compound body per section. Keep the stair flights and their upper/lower pads integral. The main compromise is chunky destruction: a whole three-floor street moves together.

Smallest useful test

Numerically check each upper stack's centre of mass before native simulation. Test both mirrored orientations, complete the full switchback path with the production motor, and test collapse in the direction with the least visual clearance.

Route experiment · Medium risk

03 Spiral Lantern

Study height 68 m · 16 main landings + intermediate rests · footprint 30 × 30 m

Open full vector structure diagramOpen full vector mechanism diagram

The build

Stack large open lantern shells containing two opposing broad ramp ribbons around a central atrium. One ribbon supports steady ascent, the other supplies passing, alternate approaches and escape.

What is different

The tower's circulation becomes its architecture. Looking through the hollow core reveals players above and below; a player can change ribbons at open landings instead of following a single endless stairwell.

How it could stand

Use four or eight broad vertical ribs with flat seats between shells. The spiral surfaces are integral to their shell; they are not loose sloping plates expected to stand by friction alone. Keep opposite ribbons roughly balanced around the axis.

How people move through it

Start with 20–28 degree ramps, flat rest/turn platforms, and a visible gap between successive ribbons. A front elevation can conceal headroom clashes: check the full capsule sweep along both curves. Leave full-height atrium escape gaps at selected levels.

What damage changes

A displaced shell becomes a tilted circular gallery or a faceted tunnel when it lands. Internal routes may remain partly useful, but it must not become a sealed rolling trap.

Cost and compromises

This is not the cheapest concept. Curved ramps and hollow ring floors need several convex pieces each. A faceted octagon is preferable to a smooth tube with dozens of physics faces; share repeated meshes and spend visual detail in the render mesh.

Smallest useful test

Build one half-turn first and walk it in both directions. Test two players passing, a knocked shell at 15/30/60 degree orientations, and debris escaping through its open sides. Compare contact count against the much simpler Zipper Spire at the same height.

04 Cross-Block Relay

Study height 72 m · 24 vertical levels · footprint 38 × 38 m

Open full vector structure diagramOpen full vector mechanism diagram

The build

Six four-storey inhabited box-girder bars alternate X and Z orientation. Each upper bar bears near its midpoint on the lower bar's broad central crossing, with independent stairs and rooftop cross-streets.

What is different

The same giant piece is a high-rise neighborhood while standing and a long covered bridge after falling. Its unusually long shape makes collapse create useful horizontal infrastructure rather than just a rubble pile.

How it could stand

Keep the centre of mass of each upper assembly over the real central crossing. Four short bearing shoes can distribute contact there. Large balanced overhangs are carried by the rigidity of each fabricated bar, not by an imaginary tensile bond between loose parts.

How people move through it

Two internal stair lanes change sides at central crossing courts. Ends provide lookout rooms and short drop routes, while a second central climb route avoids a single stair chokepoint. Reserve stair openings through the upper floors.

What damage changes

A shoe loss rotates a bar into a neighboring tower or the ground, opening a roof street and a tunnel below. Give long bars clear fall corridors so they do not collide with every district at once.

Cost and compromises

A promising ratio of occupied space to moving bodies. Long bars can still touch many neighbors and have expensive broad bounds. Limit simultaneous bridge contacts; do not populate their shells with individual window colliders.

Smallest useful test

Test a single 34 m bar on its intended centre seat, then a two-bar cross and the full stack. Check every upper assembly's centre of mass, lateral impacts, stair holes and the usefulness of a fallen bar with the player motor.

Silhouette / ruin experiment · Medium risk

05 Cage Cathedral

Study height 66 m · 21–24 split levels · footprint 30 × 30 m

Open full vector structure diagramOpen full vector mechanism diagram

The build

Three large open octagonal cage sections contain multi-level galleries and internal stairs. Deep diagonal ribs are integrated into each section, with visible loose shoes marking the separable horizontal bands.

What is different

The hollow skyscraper can tip into a large faceted arcade. A destroyed tower is still a traversable place, and its octagonal outside offers more resting orientations than a smooth cylinder.

How it could stand

Diagrid appearance alone provides no extra Unity stiffness. Each cage section is one actual rigid body; the section-to-section seats carry the load. Model only ribs needed for collision, plus a straight internal climb strip protected from diagonal interruptions.

How people move through it

Split galleries face a tall central nave. A stair lane near one rib and a second climbing lane near another prevent a single blocked gallery from sealing the tower. Large side portals give escape after rolling.

What damage changes

A shoe loss can peel one cage off the stack. It may tip onto a broad facet and become a long covered maze. Rolling direction, trapped players and high-impact contact bursts are important risks.

Cost and compromises

Very low main section count does not imply low cost: diagonal ribs, ring galleries and stairs can produce hundreds of contact shapes. This is a visual landmark candidate, not a default tower to duplicate fifty times.

Smallest useful test

Profile one cage upright and resting on three different side facets, with players and loose blocks inside. Test multiple portal exits. If collision cost is high, replace decorative diagonal physics with a simpler inner collision frame.

Best unusual mechanism to prototype · Medium–high risk

06 Drop-Stop Tower

Study height 60 m · 12 cassette floors + four sky courts · footprint 30 × 30 m

Open full vector structure diagramOpen full vector mechanism diagram

The build

Four broad frame bays contain three-floor cassettes. Each cassette rests on removable shoes above lower physical catch shoulders. Losing a shoe allows a short drop or tilt before the backup geometry catches the cassette.

What is different

Damage changes floor alignment instead of always deleting the tower. A formerly easy crossing becomes a climb; a formerly unreachable roof comes within drop range. The same amount of geometry supplies more than one map state.

How it could stand

Four widely spaced catch shoulders and broad cassette feet are real contact surfaces. Begin with a 1–1.5 m drop, large side clearances and no tight sliding guide. A one-shoe failure can skew and jam; a free clean vertical descent is not assured.

How people move through it

Keep a separate spine stair outside the moving cassette's sweep. Provide a route to both its high and low resting positions, plus an escape opening before it drops. Never put the sole mandatory exit in a crush gap.

What damage changes

A short staged drop changes connections while much of the frame remains. Subsequent frame damage can still produce full collapse. Sound and visual warnings would need to announce movement through the existing event/presentation boundary.

Cost and compromises

No slider joints are required for the first experiment. More bearings create more contacts, and repeated settling could keep bodies awake. Physical catch stops are worth testing because they worked better than an unsupported joint limit in the existing harbor.

Smallest useful test

Prototype one bay before any tower. Remove only the left shoe, only the right, both, and one while a player stands at each corner. Reject persistent jamming, ejected players, body penetration, or unsafe sole-route pinch points. Then try two stacked bays.

07 Saddle Beacon

Study height 58 m · 17–19 landings · footprint 36 × 28 m

Open full vector structure diagramOpen full vector mechanism diagram

The build

Three tapered multi-floor trestles stack on broad crown saddles. Each has two inclined side frames with open rungs, stair landings and large triangular interior spaces. Upper stages are narrower and seated directly above the load paths below.

What is different

The tower is mostly three giant structural trestles rather than hundreds of columns. An inclined exterior wall becomes an obvious escape slope when a stage falls, while the spaces underneath remain sheltered and readable.

How it could stand

Deep tied trestles are rigid manufactured sections. Broad, shallow saddles provide contact and lateral stops without tight interlocking hooks. Do not count a pointed A-frame tip as a robust support seat.

How people move through it

Stairs zigzag across the triangular void with flat turning pads. The inclined exterior supplies a second approach and opportunities to jump across the open center. Keep visual horizontal bands inside the climb plane.

What damage changes

A removed saddle or foot shoe can tip one whole stage onto its diagonal side, creating a tent-like ramp with covered space underneath. The taper makes the base broader than the upper tower without turning the entire build into a solid pyramid.

Cost and compromises

Potentially one of the simpler collision layouts. Most shapes can be long boxes with a small number of convex stair sections. Overly tight saddles or many separate diagonal sticks would erase the benefit.

Smallest useful test

Test a single trestle with the upper stage's intended mass, then all three stages. Impact across both the wide and narrow base directions. Check standing headroom near the taper and every saddle transition with the real motor.

Spatial variety experiment · Medium risk

08 Chalice Stack

Study height 63 m · Seven large courts + spiral rests · footprint 42 × 42 m

Open full vector structure diagramOpen full vector mechanism diagram

The build

Seven shallow octagonal bowl courts rise around a hollow bearing sleeve. Each bowl has walkable inclined sides, a broad annular landing, three open support ribs and a wrapping route to the next court.

What is different

The inside of the tower is a sequence of giant outdoor rooms. The walls are also slopes to traverse, and a tipped bowl can become a sheltered hillside rather than an unusable solid chunk.

How it could stand

Use broad annular or three-point sleeve seats; never stack bowls on tiny stems. The shallow shell is a rigid section assembled from convex pieces. Its centre remains open, and radial openings must connect the internal courts to the outside.

How people move through it

Target shallow side slopes and flat rim landings. A wrapping stair around the hollow sleeve gains the remaining height; the bowl's slope alone is not a full route to the next story. Interrupt the rim where it crosses an exterior climb strip.

What damage changes

An unseated bowl tilts, shedding players and debris through wide radial gaps. It can lean against another bowl to create a new ramp. Fully closed bowls are deliberately avoided because they collect bodies and blocks.

Cost and compromises

Concavity requires several physics shapes. Wide bowls can accumulate debris, so leave large spill gaps and preserve cleanup below the island. Faceted, open shells are preferable to smooth, tightly tessellated cups.

Smallest useful test

Walk the entire ascent on one bowl plus its sleeve, then stack two. Drop representative blocks into the court and verify they can escape. Profile a tipped bowl full of debris before increasing height to seven courts.

Wild-card mechanics experiment · High risk

09 Bookfall Keep

Study height 66 m · 18–21 levels across paired wings · footprint 38 × 32 m plus swing exclusion

Open full vector structure diagramOpen full vector mechanism diagram

The build

Paired inhabited ladder wings hinge to stacked movable support frames. A small initial outward lean gives gravity a known direction; removable physical wedges keep each wing at its standing stop. Selected wings can open into long inclined structures.

What is different

The flat outer spine of a building becomes a bridge or escape ramp. Its former horizontal floors become ledges and underside cover. The interesting transformation comes from which surface becomes walkable, not from an animation swapping meshes.

How it could stand

Use real hinges attached to dynamic frames, broad feet and physical stops. Design the receiving roof from the wing length and final angle; a 22 m wing at 65 degrees from vertical reaches about 20 m outward and 9.3 m above its hinge. It does not magically dock to any nearby roof.

How people move through it

Keep the standing stairs and a protected frame route usable before release. Mark the large swing volume and provide escape courts beyond it. A side that becomes the new ramp must be flat and wide enough to traverse when open.

What damage changes

A released wing rotates outward under gravity and strikes its receiving bearing or the terrain. There must be ordinary full-collapse failure if its support frame is also hit. Maximum-angle joint limits alone are not a reliable quiet resting support.

Cost and compromises

Large lever arms, moving player supports, joints and impacts make this a high-risk option despite modest body count. Start with a single hinge; keep joint-connected masses in the same order of magnitude and avoid chains of tiny supports under huge wings.

Smallest useful test

Prototype one 12 m wing first, then 22 m. Test release with players on different floors, stop impacts, receiving-roof misses, a toppled frame and a wing swept through debris. Reject persistent joint jitter or launch forces. Do not build the 66 m version until these pass.

Wild-card compression experiment · High risk

10 Kissing Towers

Study height 72 m · 24 paired levels · footprint 40 × 28 m

Open full vector structure diagramOpen full vector mechanism diagram

The build

Two slightly inward-leaning multi-floor towers meet through a large compression shim at their crowns. Broad foot cradles share a dynamic tied raft to resist spreading. Interior floors are compensated to be level in the intended standing pose.

What is different

A skyline-sized gate is also a compression arch. A visible piece at its top can change the fate of two inhabited buildings; the central void becomes a shifting canyon if the contact is lost.

How it could stand

The crown contact pushes; it cannot pull. The raft/cradles must resist the corresponding foot thrust. This differs fundamentally from copying CCTV's continuous steel loop, which has structural connections the loose game towers would not possess. A decorative skybridge does not make the pair stable.

How people move through it

Each half needs its own stair/climb route and side exits, so losing the crown does not merely trap everyone in the middle. Put the apex crossing behind a broad landing and let players see the shim from several safe approaches.

What damage changes

A removed or displaced crown shim can start inward rotation and a scissors-like collapse. Either half may also slide or topple outward under other loads. A visually attractive symmetrical fall is not guaranteed.

Cost and compromises

Potentially economical, but sensitive to crown seating, base spread and the assembly sequence. Splitting each half into several sections introduces more bearing stability work. Avoid many interlocking catches and long chains of joints.

Smallest useful test

Start with two single-body 12 m halves on a tied raft. Test crown removal, one-sided impact and loss of one cradle. Only then try sectional halves and taller versions. Measure the support reactions and actual friction behavior; do not approve it from a still image.

Ideas I would reject or restrict

  • A forest of separate diagrid sticks: many contact bodies and no automatic welded load path. Keep meaningful diagonals integral to a larger rigid section.
  • Tensegrity or cable-hung skyscrapers as the default: visually striking but dependent on tension constraints, tuning and moving supports. They add a different simulation problem before the basic tower routes work.
  • Magnetic stacking as a cure for a bad bearing: it hides an invalid structure and adds continuous forces or constraints. Use explicit broad contact seats first.
  • One enormous indestructible tower body: cheap body count but very coarse interactions. Keep separable bands and a few comprehensible weak points.
  • Every floor as an independent thin plate: more contacts, seams, tiny steps and unstable debris. Integral multi-floor sections trade some granularity for dependable routes.
  • Tight mechanical slots everywhere: interlocking detail may jam, tunnel or keep contacts awake. Start with generous clearances, broad stops and large readable keys.
  • Beautiful completely closed bowls: they catch debris and players. The Chalice needs intentional openings and an escape route before it deserves a full-height test.

The prototype funnel

  1. Start with four candidates: Sky Tree, Zipper Spire, Cross-Block Relay and Saddle Beacon. One inhabited module each, not ten maximum-height towers at once.
  2. Check authored geometry: valid closed convex pieces, no unintended initial inter-body penetration, real bearing patches, mass/COM and upper-stack support checks. Count bodies, contact shapes, joints and live contact pairs separately.
  3. Check quiet native physics: 30–60 seconds settling, at least several initial orientations/placements, and no forced Sleep(), magnetism or invisible static anchors. Record drift and which assemblies remain awake. Compare with a plain tower at equal height and usable floor area.
  4. Walk the actual routes: production desktop motor, controller/XR simulator, both directions, every switchback, top-out and equipment-restricted route. Include two characters passing and a character on the outer edge during impact. Headset comfort still needs a real headset.
  5. Test damage as level design: remove one shoe, displace a key, hit the base and midsection, then test several plausible fallen poses for cover, escape and new routes. Do not replace physics with an authored collapse and call that validation.
  6. Increase height progressively: one section, two sections, then the proposed tower height. Only raise solver settings on troublesome assemblies after measuring the effect; a higher iteration count is not an excuse for bad seating.
  7. Stress comparably: equal bots, camera, seed, timestep, wave direction and total test duration. Include a deliberate multi-tower collapse. Capture frame-time distributions, active bodies/contacts, bot planning and allocations; test on Quest before accepting a device budget.
  8. Only then try wild cards: one Drop-Stop bay, one small hinged Bookfall wing, and two short Kissing Tower halves. Stop early if the mechanism jams, launches a player, or needs hidden stabilization.

Decision rule: select a tower by useful routes and interesting damaged states per measured CPU cost. Height and low Rigidbody count alone are not a win. Combining two proven tower types will probably produce a stronger city than repeating the most exotic mechanism everywhere.
